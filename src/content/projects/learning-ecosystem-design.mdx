## The Coordination Challenge

The All of Us Research Program—one of the most ambitious biomedical research initiatives in U.S. history—required training infrastructure across an unprecedented institutional ecosystem: NIH program leadership, 100+ healthcare organizations forming the research consortium, thousands of researchers seeking participant data access, and community outreach professionals engaging diverse populations.

### Two Parallel Platforms with Distinct Challenges

**Researcher Data Access Training** was compliance-critical. Before accessing sensitive genetic data, researchers had to demonstrate understanding of ethical responsibilities. Traditional compliance training existed, but researchers viewed it as bureaucratic obligation rather than meaningful learning.

**Consortium Staff Training** operated at distributed scale. Staff across 100+ healthcare organizations had different priorities, resources, and community contexts. No systematic process existed for identifying training needs or coordinating across competing institutional interests.

### The Authority Constraint

As Instructional Design Lead for the National Affiliates & Partners Coordinating (NAPC) Center at University of Pittsburgh, I had no positional authority over researchers, consortium organizations, or the All of Us Consortium Training (ACT) Board that governed training decisions. Subject-matter experts, institutional stakeholders, and program leadership all held veto power.

This wasn't a training project—it was an institutional design challenge requiring stakeholder coordination across distributed decision-making without central authority.

## Systematic Methodology

When you lack positional authority, influence must come from methodology quality. I couldn't mandate decisions, but I could build consensus through systematic processes that stakeholders trusted.

### Human-Centered Design as Coordination Mechanism

Rather than beginning with solutions, I established consultation processes that started with listening. For every training request, we began with stakeholder conversations to understand actual needs—not just stated requirements. This revealed that training requests often masked deeper organizational challenges: unclear processes, misaligned expectations, or coordination gaps.

When the ACT Board requested a "refresher" training for returning consortium staff, consultation revealed the underlying issue: no systematic process existed for handling training updates. Rather than simply building the requested course, I worked with the board to establish governance infrastructure that would solve the coordination problem.

[See: Agile-Inspired Workflow for Distributed Decision-Making](/projects/agile-inspired-workflow)

### Quality Frameworks for Stakeholder Confidence

In distributed ecosystems, consistency builds trust. I established quality standards that applied regardless of which team member led development: backward design ensuring objective-assessment-content alignment, accessibility compliance (508 standards), rigorous beta testing with representative learners, and evidence-based iteration using analytics and feedback.

These weren't bureaucratic requirements—they were trust-building mechanisms. When stakeholders knew every training met these standards, they could delegate authority without micromanaging decisions.

### Partnership Model Over Vendor Relationship

I positioned NAPC as strategic partner rather than service provider. This meant pushing back on requests when consultation revealed better approaches, proposing structural improvements even when not asked, and maintaining responsibility for outcomes rather than just completing tasks. This stance was risky—stakeholders could have demanded we "just do what we're told." Instead, it built credibility that enabled increasingly strategic conversations about ecosystem-level challenges.

## Timeline & Strategic Evolution

<ProjectTimeline>

### 2020: Establishing Governance

**February:** ACT Board requests "refresher" training for returning consortium staff. Consultation reveals deeper problem: no systematic process for prioritizing training development across 100+ organizations with competing needs.

**April:** Working with ACT Board, I design and propose an [agile-inspired workflow](/projects/agile-inspired-workflow) that bases decisions on evidence from National Training Focus Groups and usage data rather than organizational politics. Despite having no vote or authority, the board unanimously adopts the framework. Success comes from methodology quality, not positional power.

**October:** Launch [Researcher Ethics Training](/projects/researcher-ethics-training) after extensive consultation with NIH research integrity experts and ELSI researchers from Sage Bionetworks. Strategic reframing positions regulations as professional guardrails rather than bureaucratic obstacles, addressing root causes of ethical lapses (pressures, biases, justifications) instead of assuming bad intentions.

### 2021-2022: Building Capability & Strategic Pivots

**2021:** Develop [SGM Cultural Competency training](/projects/sgm-cultural-competency) in response to top-3 training request from public library staff survey. Initial information-focused approach achieves only 50% satisfaction. Assessment data reveals knowledge-application gap: staff know about SGM identities but don't feel confident engaging respectfully in complex situations.

**2022:** Redesign SGM training with scenario-based learning exercises, interactive reflection questions, and train-the-trainer model. Following year: 92% satisfaction (only 8% say additional training needed). Evidence of systematic methodology enabling rapid pivots while maintaining stakeholder confidence.

**March 2022:** Propose restructuring Annual Consortium Member (ACM) training—required annually for all staff—from monolithic course into modular architecture (evergreen foundation + version-specific protocol). Stakeholders need immediate update launch due to pandemic delays, not structural change. Rather than pushing harder, I create demo course alongside single-course update to prove concept. [See: ACM Training Restructure](/projects/acm-training-restructure)

**Platform scaling:** TEP (Training and Education Platform) reaches 12,000+ active users across consortium ecosystem.

## Transferable Insights

These patterns apply beyond All of Us to any institutional design challenge requiring leadership without positional authority:

<InsightCard title="Solve governance problems, not just content problems">
When stakeholders request specific training, the underlying problem is often a coordination gap or unclear decision-making process. The ACT Board requested a "refresher" course—the real problem was no systematic process for handling updates. Solving the governance problem through the agile-inspired workflow created more institutional value than executing the content request.
</InsightCard>

<InsightCard title="Design methodology quality drives consensus when authority doesn't exist">
In distributed ecosystems with competing interests, stakeholders trust evidence-based processes more than organizational hierarchy. The agile-inspired workflow was adopted unanimously despite my having no vote—because it solved their coordination problem through systematic decision-making based on focus groups and usage data rather than politics.
</InsightCard>

<InsightCard title="Build the demo when stakeholders need to see it">
Strategic recommendations aren't always adopted on first proposal. When stakeholders weren't ready to split ACM training structure in 2022, I created a demo course alongside the single-course update. Two years later, the framework was adopted. Playing the long game proved more effective than pushing harder.
</InsightCard>

<InsightCard title="Diagnose root causes, not surface symptoms">
Initial stakeholder requests often describe symptoms rather than problems:
- SGM training: "Low satisfaction" wasn't a content quality problem—it was a knowledge-application gap
- ACM training: "Need updates" wasn't an editing problem—it was structural architecture making maintenance unsustainable

Human-Centered Design consultation processes revealed actual problems before proposing solutions.
</InsightCard>

<InsightCard title="Systematic methodology enables adaptation, not rigidity">
Quality frameworks and agile-inspired processes enable strategic pivots while maintaining stakeholder confidence—because changes are based on evidence rather than abandoning commitments. Both major pivots (SGM redesign, ACM restructuring) were possible because systematic methodology created feedback loops and stakeholder trust that legitimized significant changes in approach.
</InsightCard>

## The Core Principle

**Influential leadership through systematic methodology is more sustainable than leadership through positional authority**—because it builds institutional capability rather than depending on specific individuals or organizational structures.

The All of Us training infrastructure succeeded not because I had authority over stakeholders, but because systematic methodology created trust, evidence-based processes enabled coordination, and design quality drove consensus across a distributed ecosystem with competing interests.

This approach transfers to any context requiring institutional design leadership: cross-functional teams, partnership development, governance architecture, or ecosystem-level coordination where authority is distributed and influence must be earned through methodology quality rather than organizational position.
